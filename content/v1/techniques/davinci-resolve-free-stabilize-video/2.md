# How to stabilize video in DaVinci Resolve Free

Fix visible handheld camera shake in DaVinci Resolve Free. Compare three stabilization modes, control cropping, and follow a tested before-and-after example.

Source: https://voyager.so/blog/davinci-resolve-free-stabilize-video

[Watch: How to stabilize video in DaVinci Resolve Free](https://cdn.moda.app/voyager/blog/davinci-resolve-free-stabilize-video/before-after-58569a0cc0.mp4)

To stabilize video in **DaVinci Resolve Free**, select a timeline clip, open **Inspector → Video → Stabilization**, choose a mode, and click **Stabilize**.

The comparison above shows what that does on genuinely shaky footage. Watch the road markings and lamp posts on the left: they jump as the handheld camera moves. On the right, **Similarity stabilization in Resolve Free 21.1** removes much of that rapid movement while the car continues along the road.

This is real camera shake, not an effect added to make the demonstration look stronger. We used the untreated half of a public-domain handheld telephoto example and made fresh exports in Resolve. The source is low resolution; the test demonstrates motion correction, not image enhancement.

The practical tradeoff is visible too: the corrected picture is slightly tighter. This guide shows how to get that improvement, compare the three modes, and keep the crop under control.

## 1. Decide which movement you want to remove

Watch the untreated clip before opening the stabilizer. Identify something that should behave predictably, such as a tree trunk, a building edge, or the horizon. Moving water, leaves, and people are poor references for judging camera shake because they can move independently of the camera.

Then describe the intended shot:

* **Mostly stationary composition:** remove distracting bumps or rocking while keeping the subject framed.
* **Pan or tilt:** retain the overall camera move and reduce the small disturbances within it.
* **Walking or moving through a scene:** expect a harder problem. Nearby and distant objects move differently, and stabilization may make that difference more noticeable.

Do this on a duplicate timeline or a separate test copy. Keep an untreated version so you can compare the same moment without relying on memory.

Our project has four consecutive copies of the same **eight-second** excerpt. The first stays untreated; the other three use different modes. The editing source is **490×344 at 30 fps**, with its pixel shape and timestamps normalized before import.

![Resolve Free showing the untreated handheld road footage selected as the first of four identical eight-second timeline copies.](https://cdn.moda.app/voyager/blog/davinci-resolve-free-stabilize-video/source-timeline-4d7e62d516.webp)

The first copy is the reference. Judge the movement of the road and lamp posts, not the car driving away.

## 2. Open the clip's Stabilization controls

On the **Edit** page:

1. Select the clip in the timeline, rather than its source in the Media Pool.
2. Open **Inspector** at the upper right, then select **Video**.
3. Scroll down and expand **Stabilization**.
4. Choose a **Mode**, then click **Stabilize** and let the analysis finish.

![Resolve Free showing Similarity stabilization on the handheld road clip, Camera Lock off, Zoom on, Cropping Ratio 0.500, Smooth 0.250, and Strength 1.000.](https://cdn.moda.app/voyager/blog/davinci-resolve-free-stabilize-video/similarity-settings-e1dc38b2cd.webp)

These are the settings used for the corrected side of the opening comparison. The selected copy and the playhead are both in the Similarity section.

**Choose the mode before running the analysis.** If you change modes, click Stabilize again. Blackmagic's [Resolve reference manual](https://documents.blackmagicdesign.com/UserManuals/DaVinci_Resolve_21.1_Reference_Manual.pdf) explains that the mode changes the analysis, not just the label on an existing result. The manual is also available from Resolve's Help menu.

The viewer can show a different moment from the clip selected in the Inspector. Put the playhead inside the selected clip before judging its result. Otherwise, you may adjust one copy while watching another.

## 3. Compare Translation, Similarity, and Perspective

These modes allow different kinds of correction:

| Mode            | What it can change                         | A useful reason to try it                                |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Translation** | Horizontal and vertical position           | The frame mainly jitters sideways or up and down         |
| **Similarity**  | Position, rotation, and scale              | Translation leaves distracting rocking or size changes   |
| **Perspective** | Those changes plus perspective deformation | The simpler modes do not handle the camera movement well |

These are the mode distinctions documented in [Blackmagic's manual](https://documents.blackmagicdesign.com/UserManuals/DaVinci_Resolve_21.1_Reference_Manual.pdf). They are not a quality ranking. Perspective gives the stabilizer more freedom to change the image; that freedom can help a shot or create an unwanted result.

I would start with Translation when the problem looks like small positional jitter, then try Similarity if rotation remains. Try Perspective when the simpler options are not solving the problem, and compare the shape of rigid objects as well as the smoothness of the motion.

For a fair first comparison, keep the other settings fixed. The three treated copies in our test used:

| Control        | Test value |
| -------------- | ---------- |
| Camera Lock    | Off        |
| Zoom           | On         |
| Cropping Ratio | 0.500      |
| Smooth         | 0.250      |
| Strength       | 1.000      |

These are a baseline for this test, not values every clip should use. Each mode received its own native analysis and export.

## 4. Keep Camera Lock separate from smoothing

**Camera Lock** asks the stabilizer to pursue a stationary shot. It also disables Cropping Ratio and Smooth, as described in the [reference manual](https://documents.blackmagicdesign.com/UserManuals/DaVinci_Resolve_21.1_Reference_Manual.pdf). Use it when a fixed composition is the intended result, rather than treating it as a stronger general-purpose smoothing option.

For this comparison, Camera Lock stays **off** in every treated version. The goal is to reduce the shake while allowing slower movement to remain.

For a deliberate pan or tilt, preserve the overall camera move. Start with Camera Lock off and assess whether smoothing improves the small disturbances without making the move hesitate or drift. The handheld example here does not establish the best settings for every kind of camera movement.

## 5. Control the framing cost

Stabilization moves or transforms the recorded image within a fixed output frame. That can expose areas beyond the original picture. Resolve's **Zoom** option enlarges the image to hide those uncovered edges, which means some original framing is lost.

This is separate from changing the export resolution. You can still deliver a 1920×1080 file while showing a narrower portion of the recorded scene.

The controls have different jobs:

* **Zoom** hides exposed image boundaries by enlarging the picture. Turning it off can reveal those boundaries; it does not reconstruct missing footage.
* **Cropping Ratio** limits how much correction and resulting edge loss Resolve accepts. Lower values allow more aggressive stabilization. It is not a literal “percentage cropped” readout.
* **Smooth** changes how strongly the movement is smoothed over time.
* **Strength** scales the correction. Reducing it lets more original motion remain.

Blackmagic documents these controls in the manual's Stabilization section. **After changing Cropping Ratio or Smooth, run Stabilize again.** Strength adjusts the use of the existing analysis.

If the result cuts off something important, change one control at a time. First compare a simpler mode or reduce Strength. If needed, raise Cropping Ratio to constrain the correction and reanalyze. Check the beginning and end as well as the middle; a framing decision that works on one frame may fail as the camera moves.

The ordinary **Transform → Zoom** control is a separate operation. Avoid adding another zoom before you know what the stabilizer is already doing. If you also need a portrait delivery, budget for both operations: stabilization and [reframing horizontal footage to vertical](https://voyager.so/blog/davinci-resolve-free-vertical-video).

## 6. Check motion and geometry before exporting

Play the entire result at normal speed. Then inspect the troublesome moments frame by frame. A still image cannot show a sudden correction or a background that seems to stretch and relax.

| Problem you see                                      | Next thing to test                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The horizon still rocks                              | Compare Similarity with Translation                                        |
| A rigid edge bends or the scene appears to “breathe” | Compare a simpler mode and reduce the correction                           |
| Too much of the composition disappears               | Reduce Strength or constrain Cropping Ratio, then check the edges again    |
| A deliberate move becomes awkward                    | Check Camera Lock, reduce Smooth, and reanalyze                            |
| Little visible improvement                           | Compare against the untreated clip; keeping the original may be preferable |

These are troubleshooting experiments, not guaranteed fixes. The comparison does not show a dramatic Perspective failure, and it should not be read as proof that Perspective always warps footage.

Also distinguish unstable framing from blur within a frame. Stabilization can change where a blurred image sits; it does not recover detail the camera failed to record. If individual frames smear badly, a smoother trajectory alone will not make the footage crisp.

On **Deliver**, confirm the intended dimensions and frame rate, render the clip, and inspect the exported file. Our comparison timeline exported **960 frames at 30 fps**, for **32 seconds** containing four eight-second versions. Check the exported motion as well as the file dimensions; successful analysis alone does not prove a good result.

## Do you need Studio or an AI assistant?

The three modes demonstrated here ran and rendered in **DaVinci Resolve Free 21.1 on macOS**. You do not need to buy Studio or use a cloud service to reproduce this test. For other edition differences, see [Resolve Free vs Studio](https://voyager.so/blog/davinci-resolve-free-vs-studio).

An assistant can help prepare comparison timelines, organize exports, and check file dimensions and timing. In this example, Codex prepared the source excerpt and timeline file, then operated Resolve's interface. Resolve performed the stabilization itself.

If you delegate that preparation, ask for an untreated reference and separately labeled versions with recorded settings. The useful output is an editable comparison you can judge in motion, not a claim that the agent found a universally optimal number.
