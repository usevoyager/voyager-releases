# How to color grade log footage in Voyager

Grade log footage with a matched camera transform before creating a look. Includes an ARRI LogC3 example, same-frame comparisons and an editable grading recipe.

Source: https://voyager.so/blog/color-grade-log-footage

Flat-looking camera footage invites the wrong first move: drag contrast until it looks finished. You can get a punchier image that way and still have the wrong color underneath it.

Start by identifying the recording, applying the matching display transform, then making a creative choice. This tutorial takes one ARRI AMIRA shot through those three stages, with a recipe you can revise in Voyager: the log source, a normal Rec.709 view and a restrained warmer grade.

## Find out what “raw footage” actually means

Here, the camera original is **ProRes 4444 recorded in LogC3 / ARRI Wide Gamut 3**, at EI800. It is not sensor RAW. Log describes how the image is encoded; RAW describes a different stage of camera data. That distinction determines the controls and conversion you need.

ARRI's sample set includes both LogC and clips with a Rec.709 look already applied. The accompanying ALE metadata identifies our clip, `B001C003_140702_R3VJ`, as `LOG-C` with no burned-in look. [ARRI's sample-footage page](https://www.arri.com/en/learn/camera-systems/camera-sample-footage-reference-image) explains the recording variants and provides the original downloads.

Do this check before asking for a look:

```text
Inspect this camera original and its sidecar metadata before grading.
Identify the camera, codec, gamma, gamut, data range and whether a
viewing look is already baked in. State anything you cannot verify.
Preserve the original. Do not infer the input color space just because
the picture looks flat, and do not apply a log conversion twice.
```

A file's generic video tags are not always enough to identify its camera color encoding. Use the camera settings and recording metadata together. For your own footage, replace this tutorial's ARRI settings with the ones the camera actually recorded.

## Make a correct viewing image first

For this clip we used ARRI's `ARRI_LogC3-to-Gamma24_Rec709_D65-v1_33.cube`, from the official [LogC3 LUT package](https://www.arri.com/en/learn/camera-systems/tools/lut-generator). It converts the LogC3/AWG3 input into ARRI's Rec.709 display rendering. A LogC4 LUT would be the wrong input match.

This is a viewing baseline, not a claim that the LUT produces the only correct artistic result. Keep it separate from the look so you can change the creative treatment without accidentally stacking another technical conversion.

```text
Create a Rec.709 viewing version using the official ARRI LogC3-to-
Gamma24 Rec709 D65 v1 LUT. Keep the technical transform separate from
creative adjustments. Export a short comparison from exactly the same
source frames, with clear labels for log and transformed output.
Do not add a second automatic display transform on export.
```

Our downloadable recipe uses FFmpeg's 3D-LUT filter with tetrahedral interpolation. It explicitly handles the video's YUV-to-RGB range conversion before the LUT. Setting a color-space tag alone does not transform the image; the LUT is what performs the camera-to-display conversion here.

## Choose a look that belongs to the shot

The wooden propeller and patterned fabric suggest warmth. The metal structure and shaded engine benefit from a little cooler separation. We warmed the midtones and highlights, cooled the shadows slightly and added a small amount of contrast.

These are gentle adjustments after the display LUT, saved in `grade.json`. They are not RAW white-balance controls or exposure recovery. For demanding work, use a color-managed grading pipeline with the adjustments in the working space your project requires.

```text
Make a restrained warm version of the transformed shot. Favor the
wood and fabric without turning the white walls orange. Add a little
cooler separation in the shadows. Preserve texture in the wing,
readable engine detail and the existing highlight shape.
Keep an unchanged neutral version beside it for comparison.
```

Judge the whole moving shot. A visitor crossing the frame gives you a useful skin-tone reference, and the windows make it easy to notice when contrast has gone too far. Check a waveform for clipping as well as looking at the image; a beautiful middle frame can hide a bad moment later in the clip.

## Make the next revision precise in Voyager

The warmer version is a preference, not a finish line. Our second version reduces the added red in the midtones and highlights while leaving the technical transform alone. The download keeps both configurations and renders them into separate folders.

To continue in Voyager, open the recipe folder, add the camera original and official LUT, and give the agent a change it can apply precisely. Comparing the same source frame makes the decision much clearer than comparing two different moments.

```text
Keep the source and technical LUT unchanged. Duplicate grade.json as
grade-cooler.json. Reduce the added red in highlights from 0.045 to
0.015 and in midtones from 0.045 to 0.020; preserve the other settings.
Render into a separate output folder. Show matching frames of the
neutral, warm and revised looks so I can choose without guessing.
```

## Keep the transformation; question the taste

A useful grade makes the shot feel intentional while keeping the details that make it believable. First establish what the camera recorded. Then build a trustworthy display view. Only after that decide how warm, cool, dense or restrained the image should feel.

If you dislike the warm version, that is a reason to revise the look. It is not a reason to guess a different input color space.
