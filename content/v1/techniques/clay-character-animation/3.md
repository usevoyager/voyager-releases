# Bring a character to life with clay-style AI animation

Make a short clay-style character animation with a clear action and a playful payoff. Includes reference-image guidance, Voyager prompts and review tips.

Source: https://voyager.so/blog/clay-character-animation

An astronaut takes hold of a seedling’s leaf. The flower grows far too tall, and the astronaut hangs on for the ride.

It is a tiny story with a setup, a surprise and a reaction. That is what makes a clay-style character clip engaging: **the character has something to do, and the action changes their situation.** A costume and a texture alone cannot carry ten seconds.

A ten-second character-animation example from the Voyager team. Use the prompts below to develop your own scene.

This workflow is useful for a mascot introduction, a playful social post, a story pitch or a short interlude in a larger video. The result has a handmade animation look; it is not footage of physical clay puppets.

## What you need

* **Plan:** Starter or higher, for the still and the image-to-video take.
* **Tools:** nothing beyond Voyager.

## Give your character one readable action

Choose a character image with a clear silhouette and a pose that can start the scene. Note the features that should survive every frame: helmet shape, backpack, colors, proportions and any facial details.

Then give the character one action with a visible consequence. Holding a tiny leaf establishes a simple relationship between character and prop; the exaggerated growth supplies the surprise.

Start a Voyager chat with the reference image and this brief:

```text
Create a playful clay-animation scene starring [character].
Use the attached image for identity: preserve [key features].
Rebuild the whole scene in a tactile clay style, with rounded
forms, subtle tool marks and soft studio lighting.

The character takes hold of a small seedling’s leaf. The seedling
grows into an oversized flower. The character hangs on to the
leaf and is lifted off the ground, reacting with delight.

Keep the staging simple: one character, one flower, a few rounded
rocks and a plain pastel background. Make an opening still first,
with the character, hand-to-leaf contact and seedling clearly visible.
No extra characters, signs, dialogue or text.
```

The reference establishes identity; the brief establishes the medium. If the image is a photograph, make that distinction explicit so the result does not become a photographic person standing in a clay set.

## Make the opening frame easy to animate

Before approving the still, look at the contact points. Can the hand reach the leaf? Is there room above the plant for growth? Can you see where the feet meet the ground?

A clean starting pose makes the action easier to follow. Avoid a tiny character surrounded by elaborate scenery or a close crop that leaves no space for the flower.

Ask for a focused correction when needed:

```text
Keep the character design and clay material. Move the seedling
slightly away from the body so the hands and leaf remain easy to distinguish.
Leave more empty space above it. Keep both feet visible and the
leaf clearly separate from the arm.
```

## Give the motion a short sequence

Animate the approved still, spending the prompt on action and timing:

```text
Animate this approved frame into a ten-second clay-style scene.
First, the astronaut gently takes hold of the seedling’s leaf.
Then the flower grows upward, lifting the astronaut with it.
Finish with the astronaut hanging from the leaf, a little above
the ground, looking up at the flower in surprise.

Preserve the suit, helmet and backpack throughout. Keep the body
solid and the hand attached to the leaf. Use restrained, slightly
stepped character movement and a slow upward camera adjustment
so the flower remains in frame. Hold the final reaction briefly.
```

A style description can suggest stop-motion timing, but generated movement will vary. Judge the result by the character's weight and the clarity of the gag, rather than whether every frame obeys a particular animation cadence. Model-specific image and camera instructions belong to the selected model; [MiniMax's H3 guide](https://github.com/MiniMax-AI/MiniMax-H3/tree/main/skills/h3-prompt-writing) documents one such workflow.

## Check the character where the action gets difficult

Review the hand-to-leaf contact, the feet leaving the ground and the body passing behind leaves. These are the moments when a model can change a limb or hide an impossible transition.

Also compare the first and final character: the backpack should not become a different prop, the helmet should retain its shape and the silhouette should still be recognizable.

If holding the leaf fails, simplify the story. Let the character stay on the ground and lean back to look at the giant flower. Keeping a charming reaction is better than keeping a complicated action that breaks the character.

## Build the next scene from the same design

When the first clip works, reuse the approved character image for the next one. Ask Voyager for a small character sheet with front, side and three-quarter views before expanding into several scenes. Keep the same palette and material description with each request.

You can also make a shorter edit from the chosen take, ending immediately after the reveal, and export the final reaction as a still. Those derivatives reuse a good performance instead of paying to generate it again.

**Start with one character, one prop and one small reversal.** If the scene reads without dialogue, you have the foundation for a memorable little film.
